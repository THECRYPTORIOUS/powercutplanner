Plug-in solar switches off in a power cut

Even on a sunny day. The safety rules make the kit shut down the moment the grid goes off, so it can't send power down the lines to engineers who are fixing them. On its own, plug-in solar won't keep your fridge cold in a power cut.

What does work is a portable power station: a big battery with normal sockets, which you can recharge from its own solar panel during a long cut.

The rules in plain English

  • Legal in England, Scotland and Wales since 27 August 2026, under new regulations. It's not allowed in Northern Ireland yet.
  • Up to 800 W. The kit can supply no more than 800 W to your home. The panels themselves can add up to more than that, up to 2,000 W, which helps on dull days. Above 960 W of panels, the maker should advise you to consider having your wiring checked.
  • Straight into a wall socket. Not into an extension lead, a multi-way adaptor, a travel adaptor or an RCD adaptor. It must be a normal socket circuit, not a spur for a cooker or boiler, and only one kit per household for now.
  • Fire-rated panels only. The panel's outer layer, usually the backing sheet, must be rated at least Euroclass B-s3,d2. Panels aren't allowed on timber balconies, on timber or certain metal or laminate cladding, on buildings having cladding removed, or on a wall you share with a neighbour.
  • A proper mount. Cable ties, rope, tape, bungee cords and straps aren't allowed. Kits sold without a mount need one designed for the panels.
  • No batteries yet. Plug-in battery systems are outside the rules for now.
  • Old fuse box? If your consumer unit has old-style fuses rather than trip switches, have an electrician check it first.
  • Check it's on the ENA register. The kit should be listed as compliant on the Energy Networks Association's register. All the kits on this page were when we checked on 16 November 2026.
  • The full rules are in the government's plug-in solar product specification (version 2.0, July 2026).
  • Renting or in a flat? Ask your landlord or freeholder before you fix anything to a wall or balcony.

Is it worth it?

For most homes with a sunny spot, yes. The government says a kit could save a household up to £110 a year. Our calculator usually lands in the same range for a single south-facing panel, and higher for a two-panel kit if someone's home in the day to use the power.

What matters most is how much of the power you use as it's made. Anything your home doesn't use at that moment goes to the grid, and most suppliers don't pay for it yet. That's why the fridge, freezer, Wi-Fi and anything else that runs all day make a big difference, and why washing on sunny afternoons helps.

Got a power station? Plug it in to charge on a sunny afternoon. Your home uses the solar power first, so the panels cover most of the charge for free, and you've got a full battery ready for the next power cut.

How we work it out

  • Sunshine: monthly solar figures for your region, tilt and direction come from the European Commission's PVGIS tool, with 14% allowed for losses. North-facing figures are our rough estimate.
  • Each day: we spread each month's output across an average day, mix sunny and dull days, and cap it at 800 W.
  • Your home: a typical pattern of use for your household size, with more in the daytime if someone's in. Only power your home uses as it's made counts as a saving, unless you enter an export payment.
  • Payback is the kit price divided by the yearly saving. The 10-year figure allows for panels losing about half a percent of output a year and assumes electricity prices stay where they are.
  • These are estimates. Your own figures will depend on your weather, your home and your tariff.
