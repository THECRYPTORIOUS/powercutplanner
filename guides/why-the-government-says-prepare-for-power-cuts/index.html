Why is the UK government telling us to prepare for power cuts?

Updated 15 November 2026

Short answer

On 22 October 2026 the government launched Be Ready, a three-year campaign asking every household to be ready to cope for three days without power, water, phones or internet. It names cyber attacks alongside severe weather as the reasons.

What's happened so far

17 June 2026: hostile states behind most attacks

The head of the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), part of GCHQ, said around three-quarters of the cyber attacks affecting UK critical infrastructure in the past year were linked to hostile states such as Russia, China and Iran.

22 October 2026: the Be Ready campaign

The Cabinet Office launched Be Ready as the first stage of a three-year campaign. Its core advice is to be ready for three days of disruption, the same benchmark used in France, Ireland and the Netherlands. The campaign website puts it this way: "From severe weather to cyber attacks, emergencies can disrupt the services you rely on." Read the announcement on GOV.UK.

3 November 2026: the AI warning

In its annual review, the NCSC judged it highly likely that AI-powered malware will be used in a ransomware attack on Britain's critical national infrastructure by 2028. Read the NCSC report.

7 November 2026: the BBC's blackout plan

The BBC revealed its "Black Sky" plan: an emergency radio studio, with its own fuel, water and food, able to keep broadcasting government advice for at least 72 hours through a nationwide blackout.

Is a blackout actually expected?

No. The government says there's no specific threat to the national grid, and the National Energy System Operator expects Britain's electricity supplies to remain secure this winter.

But long power cuts happen without any attack. Storms and floods cause most of them. In December 2015, flooding in Lancaster cut power to 61,500 people. Being ready means you can look after your own household for a few days, so emergency services can focus on the people who need them most.

What the government says to keep at home

  • Water: 3 litres of drinking water per person per day, so 9 litres each for three days.
  • Food that needs no cooking or refrigeration.
  • A torch and spare batteries, or a wind-up torch.
  • A battery or wind-up radio for official updates if phones and internet go down.
  • A charged power bank for your phone.
  • Some cash, in case card machines and cash machines stop working.
  • A paper list of important phone numbers and any medicines with their doses.

The full checklist is on the government's Be Ready website.

Where a power station fits in

A power bank keeps a phone alive. It won't run a fridge-freezer, a Wi-Fi router or lamps. That's what a portable power station is for: a large battery with ordinary 3-pin sockets.

A typical home needs about 4 kWh to keep a fridge-freezer, Wi-Fi, lights and phones going for three days. Most popular 1 kWh units would last about 18 hours on that.
