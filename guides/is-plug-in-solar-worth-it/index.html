Is plug-in solar worth it? What it saves in every part of Great Britain

Updated 16 November 2026 · Electricity at 26.32p per kWh

Short answer

For most homes with a sunny spot, yes. A single south-facing panel saves about £90 to £110 a year depending on where you live, and a two-panel kit about £140 to £160. At typical kit prices that's a payback of roughly 4 to 5 years. Which way the panels face, shade, and how much power your home uses in the daytime matter far more than where you live.

Scotland gets less sun than the south of England, but long summer days make up some of the gap. A second panel adds less than you might expect everywhere, because on sunny days your home can't use all the extra power as it's made.

Fixing panels upright on a wall or balcony rail is the easiest option, but it makes about 27% less power than angling them on a frame. Upright panels facing north rarely pay for themselves. Laid almost flat, the direction matters much less.

Who's home in the day matters too

You only save on the power you use as it's made. Unless your supplier pays for exports, anything your home doesn't use goes to the grid for nothing. So the fridge, freezer, Wi-Fi and anything else running in daylight decide how much you save.

When it isn't worth it

  • Your only spot is an upright wall facing north, or it's shaded for most of the day.
  • You live in Northern Ireland. Plug-in kits aren't allowed there yet.
  • You rent or own a flat and can't get permission to fix panels to the wall or balcony.
  • You want backup power in a power cut. Plug-in solar switches off when the grid goes down, even in sunshine.

How to save more

  • Run big appliances in the middle of the day. Use the timer on your washing machine or dishwasher so it runs on a sunny late morning or early afternoon.
  • Charge things at midday. Laptops, e-bikes and a portable power station all soak up power that would otherwise go to the grid.
  • Ask your supplier about export payments. Most don't pay for power from plug-in kits yet. If yours does, put the rate into the calculator.

How we worked it out

Monthly solar output for each region, tilt and direction comes from the European Commission's PVGIS tool, with 14% allowed for losses. We spread each month across an average day, mix sunny and dull days, cap output at 800 W, and match it against a typical pattern of household use. North-facing figures are our own rough estimate. Your own savings will depend on your weather, your home and your tariff.
